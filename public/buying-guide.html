Buying and Selling Property in Costa Rica

Reference guide  ·  Current as of October 2026

How a Costa Rica property transaction is structured: who is involved, the steps in order, what it costs, what is taxed and who pays, how title can be held, and where the law limits what a buyer can own. Written from the statutes and government sources listed at the end.

Headline rates. The transfer tax is split between buyer and seller, who are jointly liable for all of it. Capital gains carry a primary-residence exemption and an optional flat method for assets held before 2019. Exemptions, alternative methods, and who pays what are set out in Costs at closing and Taxes by role.

Information, not legal or tax advice. This guide describes how the law and common practice work. It does not recommend for or against any transaction and does not replace a licensed Costa Rican attorney (abogado y notario) or tax professional. Rules change. Items marked In flux were changing at the time of writing.

At a glance

  • A sale of real estate is completed by a public deed signed before a notary and then recorded in the Registro Nacional.1
  • The transfer tax is 1.5% of the deed value. Buyer and seller each owe half and are jointly liable for all of it.2
  • The municipal property tax is 0.25% of declared value. A buyer can become liable for the prior owner's unpaid periods.3
  • Capital gains on a sale are taxed at 15% of net gain, with a primary-residence exemption and an optional flat method for older assets.4
  • Foreigners may own most property on the same terms as citizens. The beachfront zone is restricted.7
  • There is no mandatory licensing for real estate agents, no national MLS, and no title insurance. Agents typically work for the seller, and no inspection or verification is required by law.10

How the market is set up

Costa Rica's property market works differently from the United States, Canada, or most of Europe. These are the structural facts a buyer is working within.

TopicHow it works
Agent licensingNo mandatory licensing, training, or oversight applies to real estate agents or brokers. Anyone may legally act as one.10 A bill to regulate agents has been proposed, and none was in force as of mid-2026.11 In flux
Whom the agent works forAgents are typically paid by the seller, on commission, and typically represent the seller's interests. One agent can also work both sides of the same sale.10
Listing informationThere is no national MLS. Listings are held by individual agents and brokerages, so information on one property can differ by source.
Title insuranceTitle insurance companies do not operate as in the US. Title is verified through the Registro Nacional, and the notary-attorney carries out the title search and registration.10
Inspection and due diligenceNo inspection or verification is required by law before a purchase. What gets checked is decided by the buyer and the professionals the buyer hires.10 Notarial due diligence is often bundled into the closing fee, and its scope varies by notary.
Association financesCondominium and community fee levels, reserves, and special assessments are set within each association. Unpaid fees can become a lien on the unit.8

The result is that a buyer's protection depends on which checks are actually ordered and by whom. Independent verification, meaning a check from a party with no commission and no stake in the sale, is described in Section 11.

Who is involved

Buyer and seller
The seller must be the registered owner (or act through a valid power of attorney). If the owner is a company, the person signing must have authority recorded in the company's registry entry.
Notary (notario público)
A Costa Rican lawyer authorized as a notary prepares and signs the public deed, records it, and files the registry presentation. Either side may choose the notary, and by custom the buyer usually does. One notary can serve both sides, which is a common arrangement. Each side may also retain its own lawyer.
Real estate agent or broker
Not required for a sale. Agents are not subject to mandatory national licensing as of mid-2026. Commissions commonly range from 3% to 10%, are negotiated, and are normally paid by the seller unless the parties agree to share. An agent may represent both sides in one sale, called dual agency.
Registro Nacional
The national registry. It holds the property record (folio real), the survey plan (plano catastrado), and company records. A deed does not transfer title against third parties until it is recorded here.
Municipality
Collects the property tax, issues land-use and construction documentation, and holds the property's tax account.
Water and association entities
Depending on the area, water service is provided by AyA, a municipal service, or a community water association (ASADA). Condominiums and gated communities have an administrator and a fee structure under their own rules.

Buying, step by step

  1. Identify the property and the owner. Obtain the folio real number and the survey plan number. These identify the property in the registry independent of any street address.
  2. Review the registry record. A registry search shows the registered owner, liens, mortgages, annotations, easements, and boundary data. It reflects only what has been recorded as of the date pulled. Annotations, including preventive annotations (anotaciones preventivas) tied to a court or administrative claim, can appear in the record without being a mortgage, and they can hold up a transfer.9
  3. Check the owner's capacity. For individuals, identity and marital status. For companies, current status, legal representative, and tax standing, including the annual corporate tax under Law 9428.
  4. Confirm municipal tax status. Unpaid property tax follows the property.3 Municipalities issue tax-status certificates.
  5. Confirm physical and legal conditions. Survey plan versus the ground, access and easements, water and utility availability, river or spring setbacks,6 and whether buildings are permitted. In a condominium or association, request the accountant's fee statement and the governing documents.8
  6. Sign a purchase agreement. Often called a promesa de compraventa or option contract. It sets price, deposit, closing date, conditions, and who pays which costs. A deposit is commonly held by the notary, a lawyer, or an escrow arrangement. Terms vary by contract.
  7. Prepare the deed. The notary drafts the public deed, calculates taxes and fees, and updates the registry search close to signing.
  8. Sign the deed and pay at closing. Both parties sign before the notary. Price, transfer tax, notary fees, and any withholding are settled according to the agreement and the law.
  9. Record the deed. The notary presents it to the Registro Nacional. The registry will not record without proof the transfer tax was paid or an exemption applies.2
  10. Update the municipal account. Register the new owner for tax billing, water, electricity, and association fees.

Selling, step by step

  1. Confirm ownership and clear the record. Resolve liens, outdated annotations, name discrepancies, and unpaid taxes before listing. If the owner is a company, confirm it is active and current on corporate tax.
  2. Choose a listing arrangement. An open listing lets several agents market the property. An exclusive listing gives one agent the right to sell for a fixed period, commonly three to six months. There is no national MLS.
  3. Set price and commission in writing with any agent.
  4. Obtain documents. Registry certificate, survey plan, municipal tax-status certificate, water and utility paid-up statements, and for condominiums the accountant's statement showing fees are current.8
  5. Sign the purchase agreement and receive the deposit under its terms.
  6. Calculate taxes. Seller-side items include half of the transfer tax, capital gains tax unless exempt, and any outstanding property tax. See Section 6.
  7. Sign the deed before the notary.
  8. File the capital gains declaration where it applies (Form D-162), and if the seller is a non-resident, confirm how the buyer's withholding is handled.4
  9. Close out accounts for utilities and association fees.

Taxes by role

Transfer tax

Law 6999 sets the transfer tax at 1.5% of the value in the deed. The tax arises when the deed is executed, is due within three months of the deed date, and is owed in equal parts by buyer and seller, who are jointly liable.2 The tax authority may adjust the value if it considers the declared price too low. The registry will not record the deed without proof of payment or of an exemption.

Property tax (impuesto sobre bienes inmuebles)

Law 7509 sets a rate of 0.25% of the declared value. Owners must update the declaration at least every five years. The current owner is jointly liable for prior unpaid periods and may seek reimbursement from the prior owner. Arrears are secured by a preferential legal mortgage, and the claim prescribes after three years. An individual's sole property valued below 45 base salaries is exempt.3

Capital gains tax (Law 9635)

  • Rate: 15% of the net gain, for gain accrued since July 1, 2019.4
  • Older assets: for property acquired before July 1, 2019, the seller may instead elect 2.25% of the sale price.
  • Exemption: a primary residence, as defined in the law and regulations.
  • Filing: Form D-162.
  • Non-resident seller: the buyer withholds 2.5% and remits it on Form 129 through the TRIBU-CR system within 15 days after the end of the month of the transaction.

Luxury home tax (Law 8683)

Applies to residential properties above a value threshold, set at ₡143 million for 2026. It is declared and paid by the owner each year, with a filing window from January 1 to 15 on Form D-174. A buyer should confirm whether the property is subject to it.5

Companies

A property held through a Costa Rican company is subject to the company's own obligations, including the annual corporate tax under Law 9428 and registry beneficial-ownership reporting. Selling the shares of a company instead of the property changes which taxes and records apply. A buyer of a company inherits its liabilities.

Legal limits on land

Maritime zone (Law 6043)

The maritime-terrestrial zone extends 200 meters from the high-tide line. The first 50 meters are public and cannot be privately held. The remaining 150 meters are restricted and can be used only under a municipal concession. Foreigners with fewer than five years of residency are barred from holding concessions, as are companies with majority foreign ownership.7

River, stream, and spring setbacks (Forestry Law 7575, art. 33)

  • 10 meters of protection along rivers and streams in urban areas, 15 meters in rural areas.
  • 50 meters where the ground is broken (slope above 40%).
  • A 100-meter radius around permanent springs.
  • INVU sets the exact alignment parcel by parcel, and the setback restricts where building is allowed.6

Registry annotations and document fraud

  • Annotations are not mortgages. A claim filed during a court or administrative proceeding can be annotated against a property. It may not look like a lien but can affect whether a transfer can be recorded.9
  • Forged deeds and powers of attorney have been used to transfer properties out of the name of absent owners. A buyer should confirm that whoever signs for the owner holds a valid, recorded authority and that the registry record matches the documents presented.9
  • Registry alert service. The Registro Nacional offers a paid alert service (Alerta Registral) that emails an owner when a document is filed against a registered property. The alert follows the filing and does not prevent it.9

Water, access, and building

Water availability, legal access (an easement or a public road), and a municipal construction permit are each separate documents. A property can be properly titled and still lack any of them.

After closing

  • Confirm the deed shows as recorded in the Registro Nacional and obtain an updated registry certificate.
  • Transfer the property tax account, water, electricity, and association billing.
  • Keep the deed, registry certificate, and proof of tax payments.
  • Property tax: calendar the five-year declaration update.3
  • Luxury home tax, if applicable: calendar the January filing.5
  • Company-held property: keep corporate tax and registry filings current.

What was changing

  • Resident withholding on sales. Resolution MH-DGT-RES-0039-2025 introduced a 2% withholding on resident sellers. It was repealed by Resolution 0051-2025 effective October 6, 2025, and the registry had suspended its application by circular DGL-007-2025. In flux4
  • Setback provisions. Article 33 bis of Law 10210, a related setback provision, was partly annulled in December 2025. The Forestry Law figures above remain the baseline. In flux6
  • Agent regulation. No mandatory national licensing existed as of mid-2026. In flux
  • Thresholds. The luxury home tax threshold is reset annually.

Glossary

A lawyer who is also authorized as a notary public.

The public deed that documents the sale.
Folio real
The registry number identifying a property record.
Plano catastrado
The registered survey plan that defines a parcel's boundaries.
Registro Nacional
The national registry of property, companies, and related records.
Impuesto de traspaso
Transfer tax.
Bienes inmuebles
Real property. Also the name of the municipal property tax.

The beachfront zone governed by Law 6043.
